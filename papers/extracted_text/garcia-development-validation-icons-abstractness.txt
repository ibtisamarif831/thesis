Development and validation of icons
varying in their abstractness
Mariano Garcia, Albert N. Badre* and John T. Stasko*
Icons are used widely in human-computer interfaces. The level of
abstractness-concreteness of an icon and its effect upon performance is
of widespread interest. The authors have devised a quantitative
measure for abstractness based on the complexity of the icon. They test
their metric against subjective judgments of abstractness as identified
by two different groups of subjects. After ranking two sets of ‘abstract’
and ‘concrete’ icons, the authors examined how well the icons were
matched to the Pascal constructs that they represented. Further experi-
ments were conducted using different groups of subjects to check
whether correct matching of the icons with constructs was influenced
by context. In summary the authors found that their metric was a good
match for subjective measures of abstractness-concreteness. They also
found that there is a better identification of concrete icons than
abstract icons. Finally, it was shown that context does affect the correct
identification of icons.
Keywords: human-computer interaction, interfaces, icons,
abstractness-concreteness
Icons are graphic symbols that can be used to represent actions or operations
and that are designed to convey ideas or information in a non-verbal manner.
The use of icons continues to gain popularity in modern human-computer
interfaces. Developing a taxonomy of classification of icons is important
because it helps determine which dimensions are relevant in the interpretation
of those icons. This will lead to strategies for choosing icons for a particular task.
One methodology suggested by Lodding (1983) is to develop a taxonomy where
icons are classified by their design and the image functions. We propose
another dimension that varies on the abstractness of the representation, varying
from a concrete image to an abstract image.

icons are ones that represent the constructs at an operational level. Abstract
icons are ones which represent the constructs at a higher conceptual level. The
metric we present is based on the premise that a higher level of detail in an icon
will evoke a more concrete relationship between the icon and the construct from

Interfaces using icons to represent programming language constructs have
been developed which allow users to build programs (Edel, 1988; Glinert and
Tanimoto, 1984). Standardizing icons is beneficial because it may lead to the
development of icons which may be universally understood. This leads to
increased portability of icon-based systems and provides a common frame of
reference for the evaluation of these systems.
Rogers (1989) concludes that the use of icons in interfaces will not end soon.
She suggests that a taxonomy be developed initially to categorize the different
functions that need to be represented at the interface. Research on iconic
interfaces has shown that the care with which an interface is developed is more
important than the interface style (Potosnak, 1987; Selker et al., 1987; Whiteside
et al., 1985). We strove here to develop icon sets that were based on the
responses of typical users, which should help ensure future success of any
icon-based system to be used by users with similar characteristics. The effect of
icons on accuracy and speed in user interfaces has also been the object of study
(Kacmar and Olsen, 1991; Shulman et al., 1985). Ease of learning is also a
variable that should be taken into account when assessing the effectiveness of
an iconic interface.
The level of abstractness of an icon and its effect on performance has been
studied in previous experiments. Rogers (1986) had subjects match icons with
word-processing commands in order to show that a large variety of command
operations could be comprehended successfully in an iconic form. She also
developed six categories of concreteness-abstractness. She found that the most
effective type of mapping was that of pictorial representations depicting
concrete objects directly operated on. She also found that the least effective
icons were those which depicted the command operation by analogy. In a study
of computer-aided design (CAD) icons, Stammers et al. (1989) found superiority
for concrete icons in relation to error rates. Rohr and Keppel(l985) had subjects
perform text-processing tasks to determine how people handled a set of
pictorial symbols in comparison to a verbal command set. They found that the
group viewing concrete (functional) icons had fewer help requests than the
group viewing abstract (non-functional) icons. Arend et al. (1987), however,
found that abstract icons were searched and selected much faster than both
word commands and representational icons in menu selection for a word-
processing task.
Stammers and Hoffman (1991) studied transfer of performance between
concrete and abstract sets, finding superiority for concrete icon sets in the error
data, but no advantage from previous experience with a concrete set. They also
had subjects rank icons using Rogers’ (1986) categories of concreteness-
abstractness. In an icon appropriateness ranking study, Nolan (1989) found that
in general, respondents preferred more concrete icons to more abstract icons.

Problem statement
Our research has two goals:
l to develop and apply a metric for icons that allows us to predict the
interpretation of icons by subjects;
* to determine if the level of perceived abstractness of an icon has an effect on
the ability to identify the icon.
Icons are essential to a number of application domains. We examine icons that
can be used in visual programming, the use of graphical symbols rather than
the textual commands for representing the constructs of programming lan-
guages (Chang, 1987; Shu, 1988). In such a visual environment, programs are
constructed by manipulating icons. For these experiments, we have developed
icons to represent the constructs of a typical programming language. We seek to
compare the interpretation of these icons by two groups of subjects with
differing cultural backgrounds, to determine if there is a significant difference
between the two groups in interpretations of the icons. We also seek to verify
the suitability of the icons selected as the most concrete and abstract to
represent the intended programming language constructs. Our interest here is
to determine if there is a difference in performance depending on the icon set
which is used. Determining the effects of the level of abstractness of the icons
used may contribute to the development of more effective visual environments.
In order to achieve the two stated goals, we conducted two experiments:
ranking and matching.
Experiment 1: ranking
De~nition of metric
We have developed a metric that can be applied to icons in order to characterize
their abstractness. The basis of the metric is that symbols which are more
complex are perceived as being more concrete. The measure of a particular icon
is determined by adding up the number of the following components in each
icon:
l closed figures,
l letters,
l open figure (where the figure’s outline is not continuous),
l special characters (for example: ?,=),
l horizontal lines,

As an example of the metric, the drawing in Figure 1 has a value of 6. This value
is obtained by adding up 2 vertical lines, two arrowheads, one horizontal line,
and one closed figure.
This metric was developed due to the necessity of classifying icons as being
abstract or concrete. Previous experiments had classified icons based on the
experimenter’s judgements. The advantage of this metric is that it provides a
quantification to classify icons, instead of having to rely on subjective inter-
pretation. Various possibilities for the basis of the metric were considered.
These included the size of the elements used, the presence or absence of
particular elements (circles, rectangles, etc.) and the likeness of arrangements of
elements to common figures. Through discussion and refinements we arrived at
the metric definition presented at the start of this section.
The metric is not perfect, however. Possible problems with it are the different
groupings of components by different subjects and the possibility of creating
icons which have similar values of the metric. It is also possible artificially to
construct an icon with a great number of components that actually represents an
abstract concept. This type of construction should rarely occur in practice,
however. All in all, the metric does seem to be a good characterization of
abstractness as the following sections will show.

Methodology
Method
After developing these icons, we were prepared to present the icons with
differing metric values to subjects in order to determine how they would be
perceived. Two different groups of subjects were used. The first group of
subjects were undergraduates taking a psychology course at an engineering
school. They were volunteers and received course credits for participating in the
experiment. This group comprised 23 subjects. The second group of subjects
were undergraduates who had taken or were taking an introductory program-
ming course in Pascal at a liberal arts school in Puerto Rico. The subjects were
volunteers and received course credits for participating. This second group
comprised 18 subjects. A questionnaire was administered to the subjects who
participated in the experiment to collect information on age, sex, year in school,
major field of study, exposure to computers and/or programming courses, and
math courses taken in high school.
Materials
The materials used for this experiment were nine sets of four 4 x 6 index cards,
each showing a different icon that represents one of the nine programming
language constructs. The four icons in each set had a different value of the
proposed metric.

Results and discussion
Ranking of icons by first group
We ranked the icons for each construct from the most concrete to the most
abstract based on the subjects’ responses. These rankings were analysed using
non-parametric statistical techniques. A first analysis determined if the icons
were perceived as being different from one another. A Friedman test for ranked
data was used, with a null hypothesis that all the icons were perceived as being
the same. This null hypothesis was rejected for ail constructs in the engineering
school group with all a’ below 0.02.
In the experiment, the subjects were instructed to rank the icons from the
most concrete (1) to the most abstract (4). This meant that the icons with lower
average rank values were interpreted as being more concrete. This may be a bit
confusing because it is reversed from our metric scheme where the icons with
higher metric values were projected to be interpreted as being more concrete.
The rankings for each construct are shown in Table 5.

Ranking of icons by second group
This experiment was repeated with the subjects from the liberal arts school in
Puerto Rico. Again, the initial analysis was to determine if the icons had been
perceived as being different from each other using a Friedman test. With this
group, we were not able to reject the null hypothesis at the 0.05 level for the
icons used for ‘If’ and ‘Begin-end‘. It would have been necessary to use an a:
level of 0.06 to be able to say that the icons were perceived as being different
from each other by this group. The results are summarized in Table 6.
In this group of subjects, the icon that was perceived as the most concrete
(with the lowest average rank) had a higher metric value than the icon
perceived as the most abstract for all nine constructs. The metric predicted the
icon perceived as the most abstract in eight of the nine constructs. Again the
metric predicted the icon perceived as the most concrete in only one construct
(While). The metric did a slightly better job of characterizing the perceived
abstractness of the icons with this group than it did with the previous group.
Comparison of groups
We evaluated the results from both groups using a two-sample t-test to compare
the means of the rankings of each icon by the two groups. We also used the
Mann-Whitney U-test to compare the relative frequency distributions of the
rankings of the icons. The null hypothesis used is that the two samples come
from populations that are similar.
The similarities between the two groups in their responses to the icons is
evidenced by the high p values that the t-test and the Mann-Whitney test
calculate in most of the cases. In only 4 of the 36 (4 icons each for 9 constructs}
cases were we able to reject the null hypothesis at the 0.05 QCle vel. These four
cases were the icon with a metric value of 2 for ‘Assignment’, the icons with
metric values of 6 and 10 for ‘Case’, and the icon with metric value of 11 for ‘For’.
Also of interest is the fact that the icon that was perceived as the most abstract
for a construct was the same for both groups in seven of the nine constructs. The
constructs that did not elicit the same icon for the most abstract interpretation

Experiment 2: matching with perceived icons
In an effort to validate the icons used to represent the programming language
constructs, a second experiment was carried out with a different group of
subjects from the engineering school. We had subjects match the icons
perceived as most concrete and most abstract in the previous experiment with
the original programming language constructs. The icons that obtained satisfac-
tory identification percentages were to be included in icon sets for future
experiments.
Method
Twenty-four subjects were presented with a display on a computer terminal
that allowed them to match icons with one of ten alternatives (the nine

programming constructs plus ‘none’). None of the subjects in this experiment
had participated in the first experiment. As before, the subjects were volunteers
taking a psychology course and received course credit for their participation. To
qualify for this experiment, a student must have taken a Pascal course
previously. All subjects matched the set of nine abstract icons and the set of
nine concrete icons. The order in which the subjects saw the sets was
randomized. The order of the icons and the words also was randomized as was
the location (top-half or bottom-half) of the icons and the words.
Materials
This experiment was carried out on a Macintosh II computer using the SRGP
graphics package (Foley, 1990).
Procedure
Subjects matched icons with constructs individually, with the experimenter
present at all times. Subjects were read instructions and asked if they had any
questions. If there were no questions, the subjects were presented with a
display in which they used a mouse to select an icon and then select the
construct they thought corresponded to the icon. Both the icon selected and the
construct selected were erased from the screen after the subject selected the
construct. If the subject selected ‘none‘ instead of a construct, the icon would be
erased from the screen, but the word none would remain on the screen. This
process continued until the subject had seen all the icons in one of the sets. The
screen would then be filled again with the second set of icons and the entire
process repeated a second time.
Results
The percentages of correct identifications for the icons in the concrete set and
the abstract set are shown in Tables 7 and 8. When analysing the results for the
concrete icon set, we note satisfactory identification percentages for all icons
except the icon used for the ‘While’ construct. The ‘While‘ icon was selected as
‘none’ as the most frequent response and was identified as ‘For’ as often as
‘While’. This caused us to exclude this particular ‘While’ icon (whose metric
value was 9) from future sets. We substituted for it with the ‘While’ icon which
had been perceived as second most concrete (with a metric value of 7).
In the results for the abstract icon set, we notice that the percentages of correct
identification are lower for almost all icons when compared to the percentages
for the concrete icon set. The ‘For’ icon was identified as ‘Begin-end almost as
frequently as ‘For’. The ‘Readln’ icon was confused with the ‘Writeln’ icon.

We were interested in determining if the icons would be perceived in a

different manner if they were presented in isolation. The same icon sets were
used, but only one icon was viewed at a time.
Method
Twenty subjects were presented with a display on a computer terminal that
allowed them to match an icon with one of ten alternatives (the nine constructs
plus ‘none’). None of these subjects had participated in the previous matching
experiment. They were volunteers taking an introductory programming course
at the engineering school and received course credits for their participation.
Materials
This experiment was carried out on a Sun Spare station with a Tektronix
terminal using the SRGP graphics package.
Procedure
Again, subjects matched icons with constructs individually, with the ex-
perimenter present at all times. Subjects were read instructions and asked if
they had any questions. Subjects were presented with a display that included
one icon and the ten alternatives. Subjects used a mouse to select the construct
they thought corresponded to the icon. After selecting the construct, the icon
would be erased, but all constructs (and the word ‘none’) remained on the
screen for the next icon. This process continued until the subjects had seen all
the icons in the abstract icon set and the concrete icon set.
Results
The percentages of correct identification were fairly consistently lower when
the icons were viewed in isolation as compared to having a full set of icons
available. In the concrete icon set, there were three cases (‘Assignment’,
‘Repeat’, and ‘While’) where the percentages were slightly higher for the icon in
isolation. In the abstract icon set, there were two cases (‘Assignment’ and
‘Begin-end’) which had slightly higher percentages for the icon in isolation.
The concrete icons still had a better percentage of correct identification than
the abstract icons. When comparing the percentage of correct identification for
the icons in isolation, we notice greater percentages for the concrete icon as
compared to the abstract icon except in the ‘Begin-end’ construct. Tables 9 and

Discussion
Our conjecture here is that icons should be used contextually. People seem to
recognize and discriminate between icons better when in a meaningful
situation. In a visual programming environment, users will likely have access to
a whole set of icons rather than individual icons in isolation. The contextual use
of icons may be an important consideration in the design of icons to represent
programming language constructs.

Discussion
We found that subjects performed better with the icon sets that were based on
the subjective abstractness rankings than with the icon sets that were based on
the metric value. This suggests that the icon set that will be used in a future
visual programming environment should be based on the icon sets which take
into account subjects’ rankings.
Conclusions
We have presented a metric for characterizing the relative abstractness of icons.
The proposed metric agreed very ctosely with the classification assigned in
previous studies carried out by other experimenters. In our experiments, the
metric matched the subjective interpretation of abstract icons well, and of
concrete icons moderately well. The discrepancies noted in our set of experi-
ments seem to be caused by the perception of groups of components as a single
entity. We considered reformulating the metric to take into account the possible
perception of groups of elements but decided against this. The reason for this
decision was that it would be very difficult to anticipate which groups of
elements would be perceived as single entities. There may be considerable
variation between subjects in grouping elements, and thus this metric would
again be somewhat subjective. We decided to maintain the metric in its original
form even though the metric can sometimes generate an assessment which we
would not judge as ‘correct’. Nevertheless, the metric does function as a good

bias-free quantitative measure of icon abstractness where only subjective

We also presented the results of two experiments. The first experiment
selected icons to represent programming language constructs. The results
obtained in this experiment suggest that the two groups studied are identically
distributed around their median and mean responses to the icons used. We
note that the agreement is stronger with the concrete icons than with the
abstract icons. We interpret this to suggest that concrete icons may be
preferable to abstract icons in tasks that will be performed by users with
different cultural backgrounds.
The second experiment matched the icons with the original constructs. We
note there is better identification of the icons with the concrete set than with the
abstract set. This may be interpreted to reinforce the choice of concrete icons
instead of abstract icons in tasks that will be performed by users with different
cultural backgrounds. We also matched the icons in isolation with the program-
ming language constructs. The identification percentages were lower in almost
all of the cases. We interpret this to mean that the context in which the icon is
viewed is important for identification. We also had subjects match the icons
based on the metric with the original construct. In ten of the eleven icons which
were different from the subjectively perceived icon sets, the identification
percentage was greater with the icon from the perceived sets. This suggests
taking into account responses from typical users when selecting icons for a task.
The icons developed in this series of experiments will be used in a visual
programming environment designed for use by students taking an introductory
Pascal course. Experiments will be conducted with the environment to deter-
mine the effect of the icon set used (concrete or abstract) on the ease of learning
a procedural programming language (in this case, Pascal). Results from that
experiment should provide insight into the effect of differing icon sets used in
visual programming environments.
